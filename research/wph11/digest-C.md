**[Oct24] Q1 (1 mark, spec 21)** — Unit equivalent to the watt
Asks which unit is equivalent to the watt. Options: A Nm; B N m⁻¹; C Js; D J s⁻¹. **Answer D.** MS: "A is not correct because this is equivalent to the joule; B is not correct because this is the unit for stiffness; C is not correct because the watt is the joule per second." ER table: "Power = energy transferred / time taken."

**[Oct24] Q2 (1 mark, spec 4)** — Scalar vs vector
Which table row gives two vector quantities. Options pair: A acceleration & weight; B displacement & kinetic energy; C strain & velocity; D Young modulus & mass. **Answer A.** MS: "B/C/D are not correct because kinetic energy / strain / Young modulus is a scalar quantity."

**[Oct24] Q3 (1 mark, spec 2, 3)** — Displacement–time graph, average velocity

Options: A area between curve and x-axis from 0 to 4 h; B gradient of tangent at 4 h; C gradient of tangent at 2 h; D displacement at 4 h ÷ 4 h. **Answer D.** MS: "A is not correct because the area under a displacement-time graph has no significance; B/C ... this would give the instantaneous speed at the end of the marathon / at 2 hours."

**[Oct24] Q4 (1 mark, spec 12)** — Newton's third law pair
A cable car hangs in equilibrium from a cable. Which force forms a Newton's third law pair with the weight of the cable car? Options: A air resistance on the cable car; B gravitational pull of the cable car on the Earth; C upthrust of displaced air on the cable car; D normal contact force of the cable car on the cable. **Answer B.** MS: "A/C/D are not correct because air resistance / upthrust / normal contact force is not a gravitational force."

**[Oct24] Q5 (1 mark, spec 9, 5)** — Constant speed down a slope (N1)

Options: A component of weight parallel to slope equals sum of resistive forces; B ...parallel... greater than; C/D ...perpendicular to slope... equal/greater than. **Answer A.** MS: "B ... would cause the box to accelerate; C/D ... the component of the weight perpendicular to the slope does not affect the speed of the box."

**[Oct24] Q6 (1 mark, spec 21, 22, 19)** — Escalator power and efficiency
A moving staircase raises a person's gravitational potential energy by 5000 J in 42 s; efficiency 0.63. Which expression gives the power input in watts? Options: A 42/(5000×0.63); B (42×0.63)/5000; C 5000/(42×0.63); D (5000×0.63)/42. **Answer C.** MS: "A is not correct because the useful output power should not be inverted; ... D ... the useful output power should be divided by 0.63." ER derivation: 0.63 = (5000 J / 42 s) / total power input, so input = 5000/(42 × 0.63) ≈ 189 W.

**[Oct24] Q7 (1 mark, spec 29b)** — Elastic limit
A student stretches a thin metal wire. Which describes the wire **at** the elastic limit? Options: A elastically deformed; B plastically deformed; C at maximum extension; D snaps. **Answer A.** MS: "B ... only elastic deformation occurs below the elastic limit; C ... plastic deformation beyond the elastic limit increases the extension; D ... the wire can be extended beyond the elastic limit before it snaps."

**[Oct24] Q8 (1 mark, spec 13, 14)** — Bat and ball momentum
A bat hits a stationary ball of mass m; the bat's momentum decreases by Δp and the ball leaves with velocity v. The bat then hits a stationary ball of mass 3m and its momentum decreases by 2Δp. Speed of the 3m ball? Options: A v/6; B (2/3)v; C (3/2)v; D 6v. **Answer B.** MS: "A/D ... the momentum and mass ... should not be multiplied together; C ... the mass of the ball should not be divided by the momentum." ER: "Using the ball of mass m, Δp = mv. Using the ball of mass 3m, 2Δp = 3mv′. Therefore 2mv = 3mv′, so v′ = (2/3)v."

**[Oct24] Q9 (1 mark, spec 5, 6)** — Components of two tug forces

**[Oct24] Q10 (1 mark, spec 15, 16)** — Moments on a plank on a cylinder
A student stands on wood on top of a cylinder; feet at distances x and y either side; foot forces F and (W − F) act; wood horizontal and in equilibrium, weight of wood negligible. Which expression equals W? Options: A Fx/y + F; B Fx/y − F; C Fy/x + F; D Fy/x − F. **Answer A.** MS: B/C/D "not correct because W = Fx/y + F". ER: "Total clockwise moment about the top of the cylinder = total anticlockwise moment... So Fx = (W − F)y ... Therefore Wy = Fx + Fy, so W = Fx/y + F."

**[Oct24] Q11 (2 marks, spec 6)** — Resultant by scaled vector diagram

**[Oct24] Q12 (2 marks, spec 27)** — Hooke's law, new length of spring
A spring of length 0.18 m has stiffness 25 N m⁻¹; a force of 7.0 N stretches it. Calculate the new length.
MS: "Use of ΔF = kΔx to calculate Δx (1). Length = 0.46 m (1). Example calculation: 7.0 N = 25 N m⁻¹ × Δx; Δx = 7.0/25 = 0.28 m; l = 0.28 m + 0.18 m = 0.46 m." ER: "The most common error was for candidates to forgot to add the original length to the extension, and therefore not scoring the second marking point."

**[Oct24] Q13 (5 marks, spec 15)** — Bicycle pedal/chain-ring moment
A person of weight W = 580 N stands on a pedal attached to a chain-ring by a lever of length 0.21 m at angle θ = 24° to the horizontal. (a) Determine the moment of W about the pivot. (b) F (chain force) is proportional to the moment of W; explain how F changes as θ increases from 0° to 90° (no calculation).
(a) MS: "Use of appropriate trigonometry (1). Use of moment of force = Fx (1). Moment = 110 N m (1). Example calculation: 580 N × 0.21 m × cos(24°) = 111 N m."
(b) MS: "The horizontal/perpendicular distance between W and the pivot decreases Or The component of W perpendicular to the lever decreases Or cos(θ) decreases (from 1 to zero) (1). So the moment (of W about the pivot) decreases and F decreases (to zero) (1)."
ER 13(a): "The most common error was to use incorrect trigonometry. Candidates who used sin instead of cos could still score marking point 2... A small minority of candidates appeared to have their calculator in radians rather than in degrees... Very few candidates did not include a unit... common errors being Nm⁻¹ or N instead of Nm." ER 13(b): "Many candidates found this question challenging, with only about four in ten scoring marks... Those who attempted to describe a decrease in the perpendicular distance between W and the pivot often missed crucial detail."

**[Oct24] Q14 (6 marks, spec 11, plus 1, 3)** — Core practical 1: g by strobe photography
A ball is dropped from rest beside a vertical metre rule; a strobe (constant interval between flashes) photographs it at successive heights. (a) A mobile-phone strobe app and a laboratory strobe are used with the same interval between flashes, but each laboratory-strobe flash has a smaller duration. Explain how this affects the uncertainty in the distance fallen. (b) Distances s and times t recorded; (i) explain why s against t² gives a straight line; (ii) from the s–t² graph (s up to 0.35 m at t² up to 0.08 s²) determine g.
(a) MS: "The ball fell a smaller distance (during each flash of the laboratory strobe) Or Each image of the ball would be smaller (using the laboratory strobe) (1). The uncertainty was less (with the laboratory strobe) — MP2 dependent on MP1 (1)."
(b)(i) MS: "s = (ut+)½gt² and g is constant (allow a for g throughout) (1). Comparison of s = ½gt²(+ut) with y = mx(+c) Or s is proportional to t² so the gradient of graph is constant (1)."
(b)(ii) MS: "Use of s = ½at² and a pair of corresponding values from the graph Or pair of corresponding values from the graph used to determine gradient (1). g = 10.0 m s⁻² (allow answers in the range 9.8 m s⁻² to 10.1 m s⁻²) (dependent on MP1) (1). ER 14(a): "Candidates found this question difficult. Many did not grasp the idea that the duration of the flash had a smaller duration. The most common successful answers came from candidates who stated that the laboratory strobe would produce images that were less blurred." ER 14(b)(ii): "It was pleasing to see the number of candidates correctly determining the gradient. Some candidates did not then double this value." "A small proportion of candidates gave an answer of g = 9.81 m s⁻² but showed no evidence of any working to support their answer. These candidates gained no credit." **[Oct24] Q15 (11 marks, spec 12, 9, 18, 10, 7, 1, 5, 6)** — High jump: N3, Ek, projectile landing
An athlete of weight 680 N jumps. (a) The athlete exerts 890 N on the ground as he jumps; explain why he accelerates upwards, referring to Newton's third law. (b) Moving horizontally over the bar: (i) KE = 409 J; show the horizontal speed is about 3.4 m s⁻¹; (ii) after the bar he falls 1.4 m vertically; calculate magnitude and direction (angle from vertical) of landing velocity.
(a) MS: "By Newton's third law, the ground exerts an upwards force of 890 N on the athlete Or ... a force equal (in magnitude) and opposite (in direction) on the athlete (1). The upwards force is greater than the athlete's weight Or 890 N > 680 N Or 890 N − 680 N = 210 N (1). (So) there is a resultant force (upwards on the athlete) (1)."
(b)(i) MS: "Use of W = mg (1). Use of Ek = ½mv² (1). v = 3.44 (m s⁻¹) (1). Example calculation: m = 680/9.81 = 69.3 kg; v = √(2 × 409 / 69.3) = 3.436 m s⁻¹."
(b)(ii) MS (two accepted routes): "Use of equations of motion to calculate final vertical velocity Or Use of ΔEgrav = ΔEk (1). Use of Pythagoras' equation (1). Magnitude of final velocity = 6.3 m s⁻¹ (allow ecf from (b)(i)) (show that value gives 6.2 m/s) (1). Use of appropriate trigonometry using velocities (1). Angle from vertical = 33° (allow ecf) (1). Example calculation: v_vertical = √(2 × 9.81 × 1.4) = 5.24 m s⁻¹; magnitude = √(5.24² + 3.44²) = 6.27 m s⁻¹; angle = tan⁻¹(3.44/5.24) = 33.3°."
ER 15(a): "Some candidates did not make a direct reference to Newton's third law, which prevented them from scoring the first mark." ER 15(b)(ii): "A reasonably common error was seen from candidates who correctly determined the final vertical velocity but assumed that this was the final resultant velocity. These candidates scored marking point 1, but then used incorrect trigonometry to determine the angle from the vertical so tended to score no further marks."

**[Oct24] Q16 (10 marks, spec 8, 9, 24, 23)** — Hydrometer: free-body diagram, upthrust, density
A cylindrical hydrometer (diameter x, mass at one end) floats vertically. (a) Complete the free-body diagram for floating in pure water at equilibrium. (b) In denser seawater it floats higher: (i) explain why (3); (ii) calculate the change in vertical position given weight = 0.324 N, density of pure water 997 kg m⁻³, density of seawater 1025 kg m⁻³, x = 1.23 cm (5).

ER: "Some candidates drew three arrows, including an arrow representing drag. This prevented any marks from being scored." "A sizeable minority of candidates labelled their weight arrow with a 'G' which is not acceptable as an abbreviation for weight."
(b)(i) MS: "Any two from: The upthrust is equal to the weight of water displaced; The upthrust equals the weight of the hydrometer Or The upthrust is the same (in both types of water); The weight of displaced water equals the weight of the hydrometer Or The weight of displaced water is the same in both types of water (2). (So) a smaller volume of seawater needs to be displaced (because seawater has a greater density) (1)." **[Oct24] Q17 (10 marks, spec 9, 20, 27, 19, 32)** — Bungee jump (* QoWC)
A student bungee-jumps from a bridge on an elastic rope. Position A: distance fallen = original rope length (rope just taut); Position B: equilibrium; Position C: maximum extension. *(a) Explain how the acceleration changes from A to C (air resistance negligible) (6). **[Oct24] Q19 (12 marks, spec 25a, 25b, 23, 2, 3)** — Deep-sea diver: Stokes' law and v–t graph
(a) Spherical masses help a diver descend; one mass detaches and falls to the seabed. (i) State the conditions for Stokes' law (2). (ii) At terminal velocity the mass fell 25 m in 0.36 s; the difference between its weight and the upthrust was 4.6 N; volume 45 cm³; deduce whether Stokes' law applied (η_water = 1.2 × 10⁻³ Pa s) (5). **[Jan25] Q2 (1 mark, spec 9)** — Coin and feather falling through air
A coin and a feather are dropped from the same height through air. Which row is correct (initial acceleration / time to reach the ground)? Options: A greater for coin, less for coin; B greater for coin, same; C same for both, less for coin; D same for both, same. **Answer C.** MS: "A/B are not correct because both objects have the same initial acceleration; D ... object A [the coin] has a greater terminal velocity." **[Jan25] Q10 (1 mark, spec 5, 16)** — Tension in a wire holding traffic lights

(b) [derived] A = 8.5 cm² = 8.5 × 10⁻⁴ m² (1). Breaking stress = F/A = 330 × 10³ / 8.5 × 10⁻⁴ = 3.88 × 10⁸ Pa = **388 MPa** (1). 388 MPa is closest to (and consistent with) carbon fibre at 400 MPa — above glass fibre (320) and hybrid fibre (340) — so the blade is made from **carbon fibre** (1). (Within the precision of the data, 388 ≈ 400 MPa.)

**[Oct25] Q17 (11 marks, spec 24, 23, 25a, 25b)** — Lifting bags and an air bubble
(a) Two spherical lifting bags (diameter 50 cm each) are attached to a box (dimensions 1.10 m × 0.50 m × 0.25 m) at the bottom of the sea and filled with air. Total weight of box + bags = 2500 N; density of seawater 1020 kg m⁻³; deduce whether the box will lift from the bottom (6). (b) A small air bubble escapes and rises: (i) calculate the terminal velocity predicted by Stokes' law, given weight of air 1.82 × 10⁻⁶ N, upthrust 4.77 × 10⁻⁴ N, viscosity of seawater 8.60 × 10⁻⁴ Pa s, radius 2.25 × 10⁻³ m (3); (ii) give two reasons why the actual terminal velocity may differ (2).
(a) [derived] Volume of the two bags: 2 × (4/3)π(0.25)³ = 2 × 0.06545 = 0.1309 m³ (1). Volume of the box: 1.10 × 0.50 × 0.25 = 0.1375 m³ (1) — the box is fully submerged, so it also displaces water. Total displaced volume = 0.2684 m³ (1). Upthrust = ρVg = 1020 × 0.2684 × 9.81 = 2686 N (1). Comparison: 2686 N > 2500 N (by about 190 N) (1), so **the box will lift from the bottom of the sea** (1). (Bags alone give only 1310 N, less than 2500 N — the upthrust on the box is essential to the deduction; this is why the box dimensions are given.)
(b)(i) [derived] At terminal velocity the resultant force is zero; the bubble rises, so drag acts downwards: 6πηrv = U − W (1). Net upward force = 4.77 × 10⁻⁴ − 1.82 × 10⁻⁶ = 4.752 × 10⁻⁴ N (1). v = (4.752 × 10⁻⁴)/(6π × 8.60 × 10⁻⁴ × 2.25 × 10⁻³) = 4.752 × 10⁻⁴ / 3.647 × 10⁻⁵ = **≈ 13 m s⁻¹** (13.03 m s⁻¹) (1).
(b)(ii) [derived] Any two of: the predicted speed is very high, so the flow around the bubble is (likely) turbulent, not laminar — violating the conditions for Stokes' law; the bubble is not a small rigid sphere — it deforms/is not spherical as it rises; the bubble expands as it rises (pressure decreases), changing r and the upthrust; the viscosity varies with temperature/depth. (2 × 1 mark.)

**[Oct25] Q18 (8 marks, spec 2, 3, 21, 20, 18, 19)** — BMX: distance–time sketch and power (* part b)
A person rides to the top of a hill with enough speed to leave the ground. Stage A: constant acceleration on a horizontal surface until time T_A. Stage B: constant velocity up a curved ramp until T_B. Stage C: the bicycle leaves the ground. (a) Complete the sketch graph of distance moved vs time for stages A and B (2). *(b) Explain how the useful power output of the person varies through stages A, B and C, considering energy changes; air resistance negligible (6).
(a) [derived] For 0 < t < T_A: a curve (parabola) starting at the origin with increasing gradient (constant acceleration) (1). For T_A < t < T_B: a straight line of constant positive gradient, joining the curve smoothly at T_A (constant velocity) (1).
(b) [derived] Indicative content (6 points + linkage): Stage A — the person's chemical energy is transferred to kinetic energy; speed increases at a constant rate, and with a constant driving force the useful power output P = Fv increases as v increases (the rate of gain of KE grows) . Stage B — velocity is constant, so KE is constant; energy is transferred to gravitational potential energy at a constant rate as the bicycle climbs, so the useful power output is constant (P = mgv_vertical). Stage C — once airborne the person can exert no driving force on the bicycle/ground, so the useful power output is zero; the bicycle's KE converts to GPE (and back) with the total mechanical energy conserved (air resistance negligible). A fully-linked answer tracks "useful power output = rate of useful energy transfer" through all three stages.

**[Oct25] Q19 (9 marks, spec 1, 7, 5, 6)** — Shot put projectile
(a) The shot is very dense with a large mass; explain why the equations of motion give an accurate prediction of its path (2). (b) The shot is released from 1.85 m above the ground and lands 14.2 m away after 1.61 s: (i) show the horizontal speed was about 8.8 m s⁻¹ (2); (ii) determine the velocity (magnitude and angle to horizontal) at release (5).
(a) [derived] Air resistance has a negligible effect on such a dense, massive object (drag is small compared with its weight) (1); so the only force is its weight and the acceleration is constant, equal to g (uniform), which is the condition for the (SUVAT) equations of motion (1).
(b)(i) [derived] Horizontal motion is uniform (no horizontal force): v_h = 14.2/1.61 = 8.82 m s⁻¹ ≈ 8.8 m s⁻¹ (2 marks for method + value).
(b)(ii) [derived] Vertical: taking upwards positive, s = −1.85 m, t = 1.61 s, a = −9.81 m s⁻²: −1.85 = u_v(1.61) − ½(9.81)(1.61²) (1); ½ × 9.81 × 2.592 = 12.71, so u_v = (12.71 − 1.85)/1.61 = 10.86/1.61 = 6.75 m s⁻¹ upwards (1). Magnitude = √(8.82² + 6.75²) = √(77.8 + 45.5) = √123.3 = **11 m s⁻¹** (11.1 m s⁻¹) (1). Angle to horizontal = tan⁻¹(6.75/8.82) = **37°** (37.4°) above the horizontal (1). (+1 for consistent combination of components, e.g. Pythagoras then trig — the Oct24 Q15(b)(ii) structure.)

**[Oct25] Q20 (10 marks, spec 15, 16, 19, 17)** — Tower Bridge: moments, counterweight, motor
Each bascule section rotates about a pivot; a motor applies a moment to raise it. (a) State what is meant by the principle of moments (1). (b)(i) With the section partly raised (diagram: pivot, centre of gravity of the section on the long side), explain the direction of the moment about the pivot caused by the motor as the section is raised (2). (ii) A heavy counterweight is fixed to one end (its centre of gravity on the opposite side of the pivot); explain how adding the counterweight affects the work done by the motor to raise the section (3). (iii) The uniform section has its pivot 12 m from one end and 33 m from the other; the counterweight's centre of gravity is 11 m from the pivot (opposite side); a bus of weight 71 kN has its centre of gravity 28 m from the pivot (long side) when the section is in equilibrium; weight of section 1.2 × 10⁷ N, weight of counterweight 4.0 × 10⁶ N; determine the magnitude of the moment produced by the motor (4).
(a) [derived] For an object in equilibrium, the total clockwise moment about any point equals the total anticlockwise moment about that point (1).
(b)(i) [derived] The weight of the section acts at its centre of gravity on the long (33 m) side of the pivot, so its moment tends to lower the long end; the motor must apply a moment in the opposite direction (raising the long end) (1). As the section is raised, the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the weight decreases, so the opposing moment decreases — and once the centre of gravity passes vertically above the pivot the weight's moment reverses (now assisting the raise), so the direction of the motor's moment must also reverse to keep control (1).
(b)(ii) [derived] The counterweight's weight acts on the opposite side of the pivot, producing a moment that opposes the moment of the section's weight, so the net moment the motor must overcome is reduced (1). As the section is raised the counterweight moves down, so its gravitational potential energy decreases while the section's increases (1). The motor therefore does less work: the work done by the motor = increase in GPE of the section − decrease in GPE of the counterweight (energy from the falling counterweight is transferred to the section) (1).
(b)(iii) [derived] The section is uniform, length 45 m, so its centre of gravity is at the midpoint, 22.5 m from either end — i.e. 33 − 22.5 = 10.5 m from the pivot on the long side (1). Moments lowering the long side: section 1.2 × 10⁷ × 10.5 = 1.26 × 10⁸ N m; bus 71 × 10³ × 28 = 1.988 × 10⁶ N m (1). Opposing moment (counterweight): 4.0 × 10⁶ × 11 = 4.4 × 10⁷ N m (1). Motor moment = 1.26 × 10⁸ + 1.988 × 10⁶ − 4.4 × 10⁷ = **8.4 × 10⁷ N m** (83.99 × 10⁶ N m) (1).

## Pattern analysis (for these sessions)

### Spec-statement coverage (where and for how many marks)

- **Forces and Newton's laws (statements 9, 12) are the backbone of all three papers** — roughly 30+ marks per session when MCQs, structured parts and the * extended-response questions are counted: Oct24 Q5, Q15(a), Q17(a); Jan25 Q2, Q9, Q11, Q13(b), Q18(b), Q20(a); Oct25 Q5, Q6, Q7, Q11 and parts of Q17/Q18. Statement 12 (N3) is tested as a short recall item every session (Oct24 Q4, Jan25 Q14(a), Oct25 Q3) — 1–3 marks each time.
- **Motion graphs (2, 3)** appear in every session in both sections: Oct24 Q3 + Q19(b) (5 marks), Jan25 Q5/Q7/Q8 (3 MCQs), Oct25 Q9 + Q18(a) (3 marks). Non-uniform acceleration and tangent/area techniques (spec 3) are examined explicitly (Oct24 Q19(b)(ii) tangent, 3 marks; Jan25 Q8 trapezium area).
- **Core practical 1 (statement 11)** was examined in all three sessions: Oct24 Q14 (6 marks, strobe photos + s–t² graph), Jan25 Q7 (1 mark MCQ, gradient g/2), Oct25 Q13 (5 marks, stopwatch uncertainty + light gates). **Core practical 2 (26)** was examined through Jan25 Q20 (falling sphere in oil, 10 marks, including the Stokes calculation and a size-comparison extension). **Core practical 3 (31) was not examined as a procedure in any of the three sessions** — stress–strain/Young modulus appears only as calculation/graph-interpretation (Oct24 Q18; Jan25 Q1/Q17; Oct25 Q8/Q16).
- **Projectiles (7)**: one major structured item each session — Oct24 Q15(b) (~8 of 11 marks), Jan25 Q19 (11 marks), Oct25 Q19 (7 of 9 marks). All require resolving the initial velocity, treating horizontal and vertical motion independently, and combining components at the end (Pythagoras + trig).

### Recurring mark-scheme phrasing requirements and "allow"/"reject" style

- **Tolerance bands are printed for graph/drawing answers**: Oct24 Q11 "allow an answer between 2550 and 2650 N"; Oct24 Q14(b)(ii) "allow answers in the range 9.8 m s⁻² to 10.1 m s⁻²"; Oct24 Q19(b)(ii) "allow an answer in the range (−)0.007 m s⁻² to (−)0.01 m s⁻²"; Jan25 Q17(b)(i) "Stress = 110 (MPa) (allow 108 to 112)" and "F = 4.2 × 10⁶ N (allow 4.1 × 10⁶ to 4.3 × 10⁶)"; Jan25 Q18(a) "Magnitude = 36 N (allow 34 N to 38 N)... Angle to horizontal = 30° Or 150° (allow a tolerance of ± 2°)".
- **ecf is applied generously at the end of multi-step questions**: Jan25 Q15(b) "Efficiency = 0.27 Or 27% [ecf from (a)]"; Jan25 Q19(a)(ii) "Height above ship = 3.0 × 10³ m [ecf from (a)(i)]"; Oct24 Q15(b)(ii) "(allow ecf from (b)(i))" twice.
- **Dependent marks are flagged**: Oct24 Q14(a) "MP2 dependent on MP1"; Oct24 Q18(c)(i)/(ii) "(dependent on MP1/MP2)"; Jan25 Q14(b) "(dependent on a valid calculation for MP2)"; Jan25 Q20(a) "drag increases (and upthrust remains constant) (dependent on MP2)".
- **"Or" alternative wordings are listed rather than wtte**: Oct24 Q13(b) accepts "The horizontal/perpendicular distance between W and the pivot decreases Or The component of W perpendicular to the lever decreases Or cos(θ) decreases"; Oct24 Q19(a)(i) accepts "Small, spherical object Or Spherical object with low speed" plus "laminar flow Or flow is not turbulent". Learn the exact key words: *laminar*, *displaced*, *equal in magnitude and opposite in direction*.
- **Diagram-labelling requirements (Jan25 Q18(a))**: "Straight arrow at least 3.5 cm long representing horizontal force, with label F, horizontal force or 70 N"; "Vector triangle drawn with at least two sides in the triangle labelled"; arrows must be "in correct relative directions". Vectors labelled only with their drawn length are rejected.
- **General MS conventions** (both 2024/25 MS front matter): a missing/incorrect unit loses the final calculation mark (once per clip) but not in "show that" questions; using g = 10 instead of 9.81 costs one mark per clip (9.8 accepted); "show that" answers must be given to one more significant figure than the question value; reverse working earns both marks only in 2-mark "show that"s (only 2 of 3 marks in 3-mark ones); in practical-skill questions a specific number of significant figures may be required.
- **Conclusions are mandatory in "deduce whether" questions**: Jan25 Q14(b) and Q16(b), Oct24 Q17(b), Oct25 Q17(a)/Q16(b) all require an explicit comparison plus a stated conclusion ("...so the prediction is not correct", "...so the student will not reach the river").

### How the 2025 papers differ from Oct 2024

- **More, shorter structured questions in Jan 2025**: ten structured items (Q11–Q20, 3–11 marks each) versus nine in Oct 2024 (2–12 marks) and ten in Oct 2025 (2–11 marks). The 11–12-mark "big" materials/fluids questions of Oct 2024 (Q18, Q19) are split into smaller, more focused items in Jan 2025, though Oct 2025 returns to an 11-mark fluids question (Q17).
- **More explicit definition recall in 2025**: Jan 2025 asks candidates to "state the conditions needed for an object to be in equilibrium" (Q16(a)) and "state how Newton's third law applies to the gliders" (Q14(a)); Oct 2025 asks for the principle of conservation of momentum (Q14(a)) and the principle of moments (Q20(a)) as separate mark-earning items. Oct 2024 embedded recall inside longer explanations.
- **Vector diagrams persist but rotate**: every session has one scaled-vector-diagram question (Oct24 Q11 perpendicular forces, 2 marks; Jan25 Q18(a) 70 N/43 N at 25° with both magnitude and direction, 5 marks; Oct25 Q12 350 N/410 N at 120° with direction measured from north, 4 marks) — the skill is clearly a fixed feature, with the bearing-style direction phrasing ("from north") new in Oct 2025.
- **Oct 2025 pushes multi-concept synthesis in mechanics**: Q20(b) combines the principle of moments with a uniform body's centre of gravity, a counterweight and work/energy; Q17(a) combines upthrust on two different shapes (spheres + rectangular box) in one 6-mark deduction; Q18(b) links power to energy changes across three stages of motion. Jan 2025 also chains concepts (density → GPE → power → efficiency in Q15; Stokes → force balance → proportional reasoning in Q20), whereas Oct 2024's chains are mostly within a single topic area.
- **Terminal-velocity and Stokes'-law reasoning is increasingly qualitative as well as quantitative**: Oct 2024 required the conditions and a "deduce whether Stokes' law applies" calculation; Jan 2025 added a compare-with-a-larger-sphere proportional-reasoning part; Oct 2025 asks for two reasons why a bubble's real terminal velocity differs from the Stokes prediction (turbulence, non-spherical) — testing spec 25b understanding of limitations, not just the formula.
- **Practical evaluation is asked in alternative ways**: Oct 2024 evaluated a measurement technique (strobe flash duration → uncertainty); Oct 2025 evaluates the stopwatch method vs light gates for the same core practical (uncertainty in t is doubled in a = 2s/t²). Jan 2025 tests the data-analysis side (which graph, what gradient).
- **Marking tolerance style is unchanged** (quoted numeric bands, ecf, dependent marks), so the same exam technique (show substitution, quote to appropriate significant figures, always add a concluding sentence) remains the winning strategy across all three sessions.
