Ø Rate of reaction can be expressed either as:

Techniques for measuring rate of reactions
The technique chosen to follow the reaction will depend on the nature of the reactants and
products as well as the conditions under which the reaction is carried out.

Rates of reaction are measured by monitoring the change of an observable property of a reactant
or product over time. Some of the methods used for monitoring rate of a reaction are outlined
below.

1. Titrimetric analysis

Ø This method involves removing small samples (aliquots) from a reaction mixture using a
  pipette, at regular intervals. The reaction in the sample is then quenched (stopped) by
  immersing it in an ice bath or by adding another substance to it. Then the sample is titrated
  against a suitable standard solution to determine the concentration of a reactant species or
  product species. The titre is proportional to the concentration of the reactant or product being
  titrated.

Ø This method can be used when an acid, alkali or iodine is a reactant or product. Acids can be
  titrated with a standard solution of alkali, alkalis with a standard solution of acid and iodine
  with a standard solution of sodium thiosulfate.

2. Colorimetry (Monitoring a colour change)

     Ø If a reactant or product is coloured, the
       concentration of the coloured species can be
       monitored. This can be done using observation only
       or using a colorimeter to get more precise results,
       thus providing a quantitative measurement.

 •    For example: in clock reactions, the reaction is timed until a sudden colour change happens
      when a certain amount of product is formed.
        In Iodine clock reaction, iodine is formed in a reaction and this iodine reacts with starch
        indicator to form a noticeable blue-black colour. Rate of reaction is measured by
        monitoring the time taken for blue black colour to appear.

 •    In a similar investigation, the reaction of potassium iodide (KI) with potassium

      colorimeter.
      The change detected by the colorimeter is far more subtle than the human eye could
      observe.

5. Measuring the electrical conductivity

     Ø The principle involved is that electrical conductance depends upon the number and
       mobility of ions. If the total number or type of ions in solution changes during the reaction,
       change in electrical conductivity can be measured using a conductivity meter.

 •    In the case of some acid-alkali titrations, the end point is found out by observing
      conductance.
        Example: BrO3─(aq) + 6I─(aq) + 6H+(aq) ® 3I2(aq) + 3H2O(l) + Br─ (aq)

 •    From the end point, concentration is found and a graph of concentration against time is
      plotted to get rate of reaction. As the reaction proceeds, the decrease in electrical
      conductivity can be measured.

6. Measuring any other physical property that shows a significant change

       Ø Possible physical properties that have not already been mentioned include changes in
         the volume of liquid (‘dilatometry’), chirality and refractive index.

Rate equation

    Ø is an equation expressing the mathematical relationship between the rate of reaction and
      the concentration of the reactants. It shows how changes in the concentration affects the
      rate of reaction.
    Ø This relationship between rate and concentration can only be determined through
      experiments and cannot be inferred from the balanced stoichiometric equation.
    Ø Thus, the relationship between the rate of a chemical reaction and the concentration of
      the reactants is shown by the rate equation of the reaction which in turn leads to reaction
      mechanism.

    Ø Consider the example:                          A + 3B à 2C + 4D

         For this reaction:                   Rate a [A] and Rate a [B]




Grade 12/Unit 4/Topic 11-KINETICS /2021/semester 3                                      Page 10 of 38
Centre for Higher Secondary Education                                              Department of Chemistry


    Ø Hence the experimentally determined rate equation (or rate law or rate expression) of this
      chemical reaction is:
                                             Rate = k[A]x[B]y

                  o [A] is the concentration of A, and [B] is the concentration of B.
                  o x is order of reaction with respect to A and y is order of reaction with respect
                    to B.( x and y are integers and are determined experimentally.)
                  o The sum of these powers, in this case x + y, is known as the overall order of
                    reaction.
                  o k is the proportionality constant or rate constant of the reaction.

 The order of reaction with respect to a given reactant is the power to which the concentration
 of that species is raised in the experimentally determined rate equation.

 The overall order of reaction is the sum of the powers to which the concentration of each
 reactant is raised to in the experimentally determined rate equation or sum of all individual
 orders.

                      (*Note: The values for orders of reaction in rate equation will be 0, 1 or 2 in IAL)

Rate constant, k

    Ø The rate constant, k, is the constant of proportionality in the rate equation.
    Ø Every reaction has its own particular rate equation and therefore its own rate constant.
      Hence, the units of the rate constant will depend on the rate expression.
    Ø Its value depends on the activation energy of the reaction and temperature.
    Ø Reactions with large activation energy will have small value of k.
    Ø The value of rate constants will only change with a change in temperature.

There are two methods for determining orders of reaction, which are both experimental.

    1. Initial rate method

    Ø The order with respect to each reactant in the reaction can be determined by carrying out
      the reaction with various different initial concentrations and measuring the change in
      initial rate of reaction.
    Ø The initial rate of a reaction is the rate at the instant that the chemicals are mixed. This is
      normally found by measuring the time taken for the concentration of a reactant or a
      product to change by a known amount.
    Ø When the order of reaction with respect to one reactant is being determined, the
      concentration of that reactant is changed, and the others are kept constant so that the
      change in rate can be attributed to the change in concentration of that reactant alone.

Centre for Higher Secondary Education                                              Department of Chemistry


        Ø The experiment is then repeated, changing the concentration of one of the other reactants
          but keeping the concentration of all the others constant.
        Ø It is then possible to calculate the mathematical relationship between the rate of the
          reaction and the concentration of the reactant.

                  *Large Excess of reactants
                  In reactions where there are several reactants, if the
                  concentration of one of the reactants is kept in large excess, then
                  the reactant will appear not to affect the rate of the reaction. This
                  is because its concentration stays effectively constant.

Reaction mechanisms
Ø The orders of reaction of the individual reactants in a rate equation is helpful in suggesting a
  possible mechanism for a reaction.
Ø Mechanisms can have one step or a series of steps. If there is a series of steps, then each step
  can have its own rate. The overall rate is decided by the step with the slowest rate, also known
  as the rate determining step.
Ø The reaction mechanism can’t usually be predicted from just the chemical equation unless the
  reaction is known to be elementary.
      o Elementary reactions: a reaction that take place with a single collision between the two
           reactant particles.
  e.g.: the following reaction is known to be elementary
               NO(g) + O3(g) → NO2 (g) + O2(g),
  that is, the reaction takes place when a molecule of NO collides with a molecule of O3. Hence
  the rate equation will be;
                                                     The order indicates the no.
                                                     of molecules involved in the
                                                     rate-determining step
                  rate=k[NO] 1 [O3]1
                                                     Indicates that only molecules of NO & O3
                                                     are involved in the rate- determining step


Ø A reaction that is not elementary takes place through a series of interconnected
  steps/reactions that is called the mechanism.
      o Example: free radical substitution reaction of methane with chlorine.
         The intermediates that are formed in the mechanism do not appear in the overall
         equation but are involved in steps that result in converting reactants to products.

Ø Thus, if the experimentally determined rate equation does not match with the overall
  stoichiometry, then it is almost certain that the reaction is not elementary.

Ø A reaction involving simultaneous collision of more than two particles, all with correct
  orientation and energy is very rare, hence the multiple steps in a mechanism for some
  reactions.

                              rate=k[NO2] 2

      in the rate determining step.

Ø Hence, a suggested mechanism must be consistent with the data. The order of reaction with

 PRACTICAL SKILLS: KINETICS OF THE REACTION BETWEEN PROPANONE AND IODINE IN ACID
 SOLUTION
   Ø The reaction between propanone and iodine is
                              CH3COCH3 + I2 ® CH2ICOCH3 + HI

    Ø This reaction is catalysed using hydrogen ions.
      CH3COCH3(aq) + I2(aq) + H+(aq) → CH3COCH2I(aq) + 2H+(aq) + I-(aq)

    Ø The rate equation for this reaction can be written as
                    Rate = k [CH3COCH3 ]x[I2]y[H+]z

   Ø The rate of this reaction is followed by varying the concentration of each species and then
       titrating the remaining unreacted iodine with standard sodium thiosulfate using starch
       indicator. Alternatively, a colorimeter can be used to monitor the brown colour of the
       iodine.
   Ø Experimental data shows the reaction to be first order with respect to both propanone and
       hydrogen ions, but zero order for iodine. The reactant iodine is not in the rate equation at
       all but hydrogen ions are present, despite not being reactants. Hence for this reaction the
       rate equation is: Rate = k [CH3COCH3] [H+]
A possible mechanism for this reaction in the presence of an acid catalyst is:
       CH3COCH3 + H+ ® ( CH3C(OH)CH3)+                    step 1 (slow)
       (CH3C(OH)CH3) ® CH2C(OH)CH3 + H
                       +                          +
                                                          step 2 (fast)
       CH2C(OH)CH3 + I2 ® CH2IC(OH)(I)CH3                 step 3 (fast)
       CH2IC(OH)(I)CH3 ® CH2ICOCH3 + H + I   +     -
                                                          step 4 (fast)

    o Propanone and H+ are in rate determining step, hence they are in the rate equation.
    o Orders of reaction with respect to these two are 1, so the rate determining step uses one
      molecule of each.
    o Order with respect to iodine is 0 hence it is not involved in the rate determining step and it
      is not included in the rate equation.

Why do H+ ions appear in the rate equation?
  o The reaction is catalysed by acid [H+] which affects the rate of the reaction but is
      unchanged overall.
  o Catalysts appear in the rate equation because they affect the rate, but they do not appear
      in the stoichiometric equation because they remain chemically unchanged.

    Ø Activation energy, Ea : minimum amount of energy that colliding particles must possess for
      a reaction to occur.


    Catalysts
    Ø A substance that increase the rate of reaction by providing an alternative reaction route
      with lower activation energy and is chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction.
    Ø The energy profile diagram for an exothermic reaction show how catalysts work.




    Ø Catalysts can be divided into two types:
      o Homogenous catalysts and
      o Heterogenous catalysts

Homogeneous catalysts
   Ø A homogeneous catalyst is in the same phase (solid, liquid, solution or gas) as the
      reactants
Example:
   a) iodination of propanone. H+ (aq) ion catalyses the reaction
      CH3COCH3 (aq) + I2 (aq) ® CH3COCH2I (aq)+ H+ (aq) + I- (aq)

    b) a gaseous catalyst in a mixture of gases or a liquid catalyst in a mixture of liquids.
       Chlorine free radicals act as a homogeneous catalyst in the gas phase.
              Cl• (g) + O3(g) → ClO• (g) + O2(g)
              ClO• (g) + O3 (g) → Cl• (g) + 2O2(g)

Heterogeneous catalysts
   Ø A Heterogeneous catalyst is in a different phase to that of the reactants.
      Example: in the Haber process the solid iron catalyst catalyses the reaction between two
      gases, hydrogen and nitrogen.

    Ø It takes place at active sites on the surface of a solid (e.g. a metal). Gases are adsorbed
      onto the surface and form weak bonds with metal atoms. This form of catalysis is thought
      to work in three stages as follows:

    1. Adsorption: reactants adsorbed (the adhesion of atoms, molecules or ions to the surface
       of a solid) onto the surface of the catalyst. Formation of bonds with the metal may use
       some of the electrons from bonds within the gas molecules thus weakening these bonds.




    2. Reaction: reactant molecules are held in position that enable them to react together




    3. Desorption: the product molecules leave the surface/active sites.




*The rate of reaction is controlled by how fast the reactants are adsorbed and how fast the
products are desorbed.
