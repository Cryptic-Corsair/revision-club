# Digest A — Oct 2022 + Jan 2023

**Sources.** Official Pearson Edexcel IAL WPH11/01 (Unit 1: Mechanics and Materials) question papers, mark schemes (MS) and Principal Examiners' Reports (ER) for October 2022 (paper P71878A) and January 2023 (P71864A). Both papers: 1 h 30 min, 80 marks — Section A = Q1–10 multiple choice (1 mark each, A–D), Section B = 70 marks of structured questions. Oct 2022 has **nine** structured questions (Q11–Q19); Jan 2023 has eight (Q11–Q18). Spec-statement numbers (1–32) follow the WPH11 list. MP = marking point, IC = indicative content, ecf = error carried forward. Diagram-only MCQ options are described from the text extraction; radicals lost in [Oct22] Q9 are restored from the physics.

## Question-by-question

### October 2022 (P71878A)

**[Oct22] Q1 (1 mark, spec 3) — Area under a velocity–time graph.** Which quantity is given by the area under a velocity–time graph? A acceleration, B displacement, C speed, D time taken. **Answer B.** MS: "A is incorrect because acceleration is rate of change of velocity. C is incorrect because speed is the magnitude of velocity. D is incorrect because time taken is the x-axis variable."

**[Oct22] Q2 (1 mark, spec 4) — Vector quantity.** Which is a vector? A energy, B mass, C momentum, D power. **Answer C** (MS: energy, mass and power are not vectors).

**[Oct22] Q3 (1 mark, spec 25b) — Conditions for Stokes' law.** When does Stokes' law apply for objects moving in a fluid? A all spherical objects moving at low speeds; B all spherical objects in a fluid with low viscosity; C small spherical objects moving at low speeds; D small spherical objects in a fluid with low viscosity. **Answer C.** MS: it "does not apply to all spheres" and "applies at all viscosities".

**[Oct22] Q4 (1 mark, specs 2, 3) — Acceleration–time to velocity–time graph.** A car accelerated along a straight road; the graph shows acceleration varying (decreasing) with time. Choose the v–t graph over the same interval. **Answer A** (velocity rising with a gradient that follows the falling acceleration). MS: "B is incorrect because the gradient is zero except at one point. C is incorrect because the gradient begins negative and becomes positive. D is incorrect because the positive and negative gradients are constant except at one point."

**[Oct22] Q5 (1 mark, spec 28) — Meaning of a large Young modulus.** A wire material has a large Young modulus; which best describes it? A a large force per unit cross-sectional area is required to break the wire; B the mass per unit volume is large; C the ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain is large; D after removing a large stress the sample returns to its original length. **Answer C.** MS: Young modulus "is not the breaking stress … not the density … not the elastic limit of a material".

**[Oct22] Q6 (1 mark, specs 29a, 32) — Work from a force–extension graph.** A rubber band is stretched by an increasing force, then the force is decreased and the band returns to its original length; areas P (above the loading curve), Q and R partition the region under the curves. Which gives the work done in stretching up to maximum extension? A P+Q, B Q+R, C R, D Q. **Answer B.** MS: "A is incorrect because P is spurious and R is not included. C is incorrect because Q is not included. D is incorrect because R is not included."

**[Oct22] Q7 (1 mark, spec 6) — Resultant of two coplanar vectors.** Two forces X and Y act at a point; four vector diagrams show X, Y and a labelled resultant force. **Answer C** (X and Y correctly added nose-to-tail). MS: "A & B are incorrect because the vectors have not been added. D is incorrect because the resultant is in the wrong direction."

**[Oct22] Q8 (1 mark, specs 27, 32) — Elastic energy of springs in parallel.** One spring stretched by tensile force F stores elastic energy E; three such springs in parallel are stretched by 2F. Energy stored in the system = ? A (1/3)E, B (2/3)E, C (4/3)E, D 2E. **Answer C.** MS: A/B wrong because "the force has been neglected, or the extension is wrong"; D wrong because "the extension has been neglected". ER: "Twice the force for two thirds of the extension gives four thirds of the energy"; Q8 "was the only question that caused candidates significant difficulty" in Section A.

**[Oct22] Q9 (1 mark, spec 18) — Equal kinetic energies, speed ratio.** A proton and an electron have equal kinetic energies; the proton's speed is v; the proton's mass is 2000 times the electron's. Electron speed = ? A √2000 × v, B v/2000, C v/√2000, D 2000 × v (radicals lost in the text extraction). **Answer A.** MS: B and C wrong "because the electron is lighter than the proton"; D wrong because "the velocity is not inversely proportional to mass". ER: "The ratio of the masses is the inverse square of the ratio of the velocities."

**[Oct22] Q10 (1 mark, specs 21, 22) — Dissipated power from efficiency.** A pump has output power 25 kW and efficiency 52%; which expression gives the rate in kW at which energy is dissipated? A 0.48 × 25; B 0.52 × 25; C (0.48 × 25)/0.52; D 25/0.52. **Answer C.** MS: A gives "48% of only the power output", B "52% of the power output", D "the total power input". ER: "Energy wasted is (1 − efficiency) × total power input."

**[Oct22] Q11 (5 marks, specs 9, 21) — The Motorboat.** The engine provides a constant horizontal force of 5.5 kN; as the boat's velocity increases the resultant horizontal force decreases until it travels at terminal velocity.
- (a) State what is meant by a resultant force. (1) MS: "The (vector) sum of all forces (acting on an object) Or The single force that would have the same effect as all the other forces acting together (1). [Treat 'net force' as synonym for 'resultant force', so no mark]."
- (b) At a certain time the drag force is 3.1 kN; mass of boat = 7.5 × 10³ kg; calculate the acceleration. (2) MS: "Use of F = ma (1) [allow 3.1 kN or 5.5 kN (0.41 or 0.73 m s⁻² respectively)]; a = 3.2 × 10⁻¹ m s⁻² (1)." Example: "(5.5 – 3.1) × 10³ N = 7.5 × 10³ kg × a; a = 2.4 × 10³ N ÷ 7.5 × 10³ kg = 0.32 m s⁻²."
- (c) Terminal velocity = 4.8 m s⁻¹; calculate the output power of the engine. (2) MS: "Use of P = W/t and ΔW = FΔx [allow P = Fv] (1); [allow 2.4 kN or 3.1 kN (1.2 × 10⁴ or 1.5 × 10⁴ W respectively)]; P = 2.6 × 10⁴ W [or J s⁻¹] (1)." Example: "P = W/t = FΔx/t = Fv = 5.5 × 10³ N × 4.8 m s⁻¹ = 2.64 × 10⁴ W."
ER Q11(b): "It was common for candidates to use an incorrect value for resultant force due to forgetting that two unequal horizontal forces were acting on the boat." ER Q11(c): "Many candidates attempted to calculate the kinetic energy of the boat, which is irrelevant."

**[Oct22] Q12 (7 marks, specs 28, 30, 31, 27, 29a) — The Constantan Wire.** A student determines the Young modulus of constantan; wire cross-sectional area A = 3.97 × 10⁻⁷ m², unstretched length x = 4.00 m.
- (a) The student can apply a weight of up to 150 N; the breaking stress of constantan is about 420 MPa. Deduce whether the wire could support 150 N. (3) MS: "Use of σ = F/A (1); σ = 3.8 × 10⁸ Pa [accept N m⁻²] Or F_b = 170 N Or A_min = 3.6 × 10⁻⁷ m² (1); Valid comparison in consistent units and conclusion (1)." Example: "σ = 150 N ÷ 3.97 × 10⁻⁷ m² = 3.78 × 10⁸ Pa; 3.78 < 4.20 ∴ will not break."
- (b) Slotted masses added, extensions measured, force–extension graph plotted (force 0–100 N, extension 0–10 mm; straight line up to 5 mm then curving).
  - (i) Show that the stiffness is about 1.3 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹. (2) MS: "Determine gradient of straight line section [straight line ends at 5 mm] (1); [Δx ≥ 3 mm for gradient][Allow use of tangent at origin] (1); k = 1.30 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹ [1.27 to 1.33][need to see third s.f.]." Example: "gradient = 60/4.6 = 13.0 N mm⁻¹ = 1.30 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹." ER: "many candidates did not obtain a sufficiently accurate value of the gradient to score all the marks. Greater emphasis on interpretation of graphs would improve performance in this type of question."
  - (ii) Given k = EA/x, determine the Young modulus. (2) MS: "Use of k = EA/x (1); E = 1.3 × 10¹¹ Pa (ecf from (b)(i)) [their (b)(i) × 1.01 × 10⁷ + unit] (1)." Example: "E = kx/A = 1.3 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹ × 4.00 ÷ 3.97 × 10⁻⁷ m² = 1.3 × 10¹¹ Pa."

**[Oct22] Q13 (7 marks, specs 15, 16, 8) — The Wheelbarrow.** A woman supports a stationary wheelbarrow with an upward force P on the handles; an upward force R from the ground acts on the wheel; the centre of gravity G lies between them.
- (a) Explain, by considering moments about G, why P is less than R. (3) MS: "Moments due to force on wheel and force on handle must be equal (magnitude about any point) (1); Moment is force times (perpendicular) distance [accept 'Fx' but no other symbols unless in question or defined by candidate] (1); Handle is further from centre of gravity than wheel (so less force for equal moment) (1) [NB independent mark]."
- (b) Horizontal distance wheel-centre to handles = 1.5 m; P = 80 N, R = 320 N. Determine the horizontal distance between the centre of gravity and the centre of the wheel. (4) MS: "Uses weight = 400 N Or Uses x and (1.5 − x) (1); Use of moment = Fx about a stated point (1) [accept pivot point clearly indicated on diagram]; Use of principle of moments (1); x = 0.3 m (1)." Example: "Weight = 320 + 80 = 400 N; Taking moments about line of action of 320 N force: 400 N × x = 80 N × 1.5 m; x = 120 Nm ÷ 400 N = 0.30 m." ER: "A majority of candidates were able to calculate the distance correctly, but a large number missed a mark by not specifying a pivot point. Any point can be taken as a pivot point, the C of G and the wheel being the obvious ones."

**[Oct22] Q14 (10 marks, specs 23, 24, 9, 10) — The Lost Balloon.** A child releases a helium balloon (less dense than air); mass of balloon and string = 9.20 × 10⁻³ kg, volume = 5.00 × 10⁻² m³, density of air = 1.20 kg m⁻³.
- (a) Calculate the resultant force on the balloon immediately after release. (4) MS: "Use of upthrust = weight of fluid displaced (1); Use of ρ = m/V [accept use to calculate density of balloon, 0.184 kg m⁻³] (1); [Correct use of ρ_air gV to find resultant can score MP1 and MP2]; Use of W = mg (1); Resultant force = 0.5 N (1)." Example: "U = 0.05 m³ × 1.20 kg m⁻³ × 9.81 N kg⁻¹ = 0.589 N; W = 9.20 × 10⁻³ kg × 9.81 N kg⁻¹ = 0.090 N; Resultant force = U − W = 0.589 N − 0.090 N = 0.498 N." ER: "A number worked out the density of the balloon, which was not required."
- *(b) Air density constant, no wind: explain how the forces on the balloon affect its motion as it moves upwards from the moment it is released. (6, QoWC asterisk; IC + linkage table — 6 IC points → 4 marks, max 2 linkage marks.)** IC: "Initially there is a resultant upward force Or Upthrust is greater than weight and initially there is no air resistance [accept 'drag']"; "Balloon accelerates (upwards) Or … with increasing velocity/speed"; "(Downward) air resistance force, (initially zero) increases as velocity/speed increases. (U and W are constant.)"; "Resultant (upward) force decreases so acceleration decreases"; "(Eventually) resultant force is zero Or upthrust = weight + drag Or the forces (on the balloon) are balanced"; "Balloon continues to move (upwards) at constant velocity Or … with terminal velocity/speed." [Allow "net force" as synonym for "resultant force"; accept U, W.] ER: "In many responses, it wasn't clear whether comments were referring to the initial stage or the terminal velocity stage"; "Terminal velocity situations are commonly asked about on exams so should be sufficiently covered."

**[Oct22] Q15 (7 marks, specs 7, 5, 1) — The Ball and the Wall.** A boy throws a ball at a wall 5.00 m away; the top of the wall is 3.00 m above the release point; initial velocity 9.70 m s⁻¹ at 49.0° to the horizontal.
- (a) Show that the ball travelled the distance to the wall in about 0.8 s. (3) MS: "Use of correct trigonometry to calculate horizontal component [9.7 cos 49° or 9.7 sin 41° seen] (1); Use of s = ut + ½at² with a = 0 [i.e. use of s = vt] (1); t = 0.79 s (1) [NB reverse argument scores 2 marks (Rule 4.2)]." Example: "v_H = 9.70 m s⁻¹ × cos 49° = 6.36 m s⁻¹; t = 5.00 m ÷ 6.36 m s⁻¹ = 0.786 s."
- (b) Deduce whether or not the ball went over the wall. (4) MS: "Use of correct trigonometry to calculate vertical component [9.7 sin 49° or 9.7 cos 41° seen] (1); Use of s = ut + ½at² (1); s = 2.7 m (ecf from (a)) (1) ['show that' value also gives 2.72 m]; Correct conclusion from valid comparison using student's calculated value (1). Or Use of v² = u² + 2as → Max height = 2.7 m [no ecf] … [allow any valid suvat method, allow ecf if method involves t from (a)]." Example: "v_V = 9.70 × sin 49° = 7.32 m s⁻¹; s = 7.32 × 0.79 − 0.5 × 9.81 × (0.79)² = 2.72 m; 2.72 m < 3.00 m so ball does not go over the wall." ER: "Many candidates lost marks by neglecting units for their values of height."

**[Oct22] Q16 (7 marks, specs 14, 13, 20/18) — The Skaters.** Skater B (60 kg, 7.5 m s⁻¹) collides with skater A (65 kg, 5.5 m s⁻¹) directly ahead and locks his arms around A; they move on together at 6.2 m s⁻¹.
- (a) State the principle of conservation of momentum. (2) MS: "Sum of momenta before (collision) = sum of momenta after (collision) Or Total momentum remains constant (1); Provided no external/unbalanced/resultant force acts (on the system) Or in a closed/isolated system (1)." ER: "Candidates typically failed to score the second mark by neglecting to state the condition for momentum conservation. Students should be encouraged to learn good forms of words for standard answers."
- (b) Deduce whether momentum is conserved in this collision. (3) MS: "Use of p = mv (1); Momentum before collision = 810 N s and after collision = 780 N s Or Expected velocity = 6.5 m s⁻¹ (1); Correct conclusion based on comparison of candidate's momenta/speeds (1)." Example: "before: 65 × 5.5 + 60 × 7.5 = 807.5 N s; after: (65 + 60) × 6.2 = 775.0 N s; 775 ≠ 808 ∴ momentum not conserved."
- (c) The KE after the collision is much less than before, so the collision is inelastic; explain the decrease in kinetic energy. (2) MS: "Forces acted between skaters (during the collision) Or External forces [accept friction (between skates and ice)] act on the skaters (1); Work done (by forces) during the collision was not recovered Or … was dissipated Or … transfers (kinetic) energy to thermal energy [accept 'heat'] (1)." ER: "no marks can be easily awarded without mentioning work being done. Candidates overall simply stated that energy was transformed without telling us how."

**[Oct22] Q17 (9 marks, specs 25a, 25b, 24, 9, 10) — The Foraminifera.** Forams (small sea creatures) sink slowly through seawater when they die.
- (a)(i) A spherical foram of mass 1.15 × 10⁻⁸ kg sinks at terminal velocity; upthrust from the seawater = 4.37 × 10⁻⁸ N; flow is laminar. Show the viscous drag is about 7 × 10⁻⁸ N. (3) MS: "Use of W = mg (1); Use of Newton first law (1); 6.9 × 10⁻⁸ N (1)." Example: "W = 1.15 × 10⁻⁸ kg × 9.81 N kg⁻¹ = 1.13 × 10⁻⁷ N; D = W − U = 1.13 × 10⁻⁷ N − 4.37 × 10⁻⁸ N = 6.91 × 10⁻⁸ N."
- (a)(ii) Calculate the terminal velocity. diameter = 9.2 × 10⁻⁴ m; viscosity of seawater = 1.41 × 10⁻³ Pa s. (2) MS: "Use of F = 6πηrv [allow diameter for radius] (1); Terminal velocity = 5.7 × 10⁻³ m s⁻¹ (ecf from (a)(i)) (1) ['show that' value gives 5.73 × 10⁻³ m s⁻¹]." Example: "D = 6π × 1.41 × 10⁻³ Pa s × 4.6 × 10⁻⁴ m × v = 6.91 × 10⁻⁸ N; v = 6.91 × 10⁻⁸ ÷ (6π × 1.41 × 10⁻³ × 4.6 × 10⁻⁴) = 5.65 × 10⁻³ m s⁻¹."
- (b) As the forams sink deeper the water becomes colder. Discuss how the terminal velocities change as they sink deeper. (4) MS: "Viscosity increases (with lower temperature) so drag force increases (for given velocity) OR … so (terminal) velocity slower for given drag force [allow reference to F = 6πηρv] (1); Density increases (with increasing depth) so upthrust increases [ignore 'upthrust is constant'] (1); Weight remains constant [do not accept 'mass'] (1); Terminal velocity reduces (with increasing depth) (dependent on MP1 or MP2) (1)." ER: "Many candidates did not realise that the drag force at terminal velocity remains the same, and that the velocity can decrease to provide sufficient drag if the viscosity becomes greater."

**[Oct22] Q18 (9 marks, specs 8, 12) — The Shunting Engine.** An engine pushes a truck along horizontal rails at constant velocity; the engine exerts a forward force of 1.2 × 10⁵ N on the truck and a net backwards force of 1.5 × 10⁵ N on the rails.
- (a) Complete the free-body force diagram for the engine (normal contact force and weight already drawn). (4) MS: "Force from truck to the left [accept 1.2 × 10⁵ N] (1); Air resistance to the left [accept 3.0 × 10⁴ N][do not accept 'viscous drag'] (1); Force from rails to the right [accept 1.5 × 10⁵ N] (1); [withhold one mark if more forces than three][if magnitudes used ignore names of forces]; Total length of arrows towards the left equals length of arrow to the right [to within 2 mm using measuring tool][dependent on any three horizontal forces] (1)." ER: "Many students failed to notice that an additional backwards force from air resistance was required to bring the engine into equilibrium. The arrows in a free-body force diagram should have lengths proportional to the magnitudes of the forces. This was very rarely seen."
- (b) A student suggests the weight and normal reaction force form a Newton's third law pair. Explain why this is incorrect, referring to the features of an N3 pair. (5) MS: "Forces of equal magnitude that act in opposite directions [do not accept 'equal and opposite reaction'][accept act for equal times] (1); Same type of force (1); (Acting on) different bodies (1); These two forces both act on the engine [accept 'on the same body'] (1); One force is gravitational [do not accept 'weight'] and the other is a contact force (1)." ER: "Details of why the forces are not of the same nature required an account of what the natures of the forces are."

**[Oct22] Q19 (9 marks, specs 17, 19, 20) — The Cyclist on the Hill.** A cyclist moves up a 4.0° slope at constant speed; constant forward force 150 N; distance along the slope 215 m; mass of cyclist and bicycle = 90 kg.
- (a) Show that the work done by the cyclist is about 3 × 10⁴ J. (2) MS: "Use of ΔW = FΔx [allow any dimensionally correct variation, e.g. involving trig] (1); ΔW = 3.2 × 10⁴ J [do not allow if cos 4° used in MP1, gives 3.217] (1)." Example: "ΔW = 150 N × 215 m = 3.23 × 10⁴ J." ER: "A common mistake was to multiply the distance by a cosine or a sine of the angle."
- (b)(i) The cyclist does work against gravity and air resistance; calculate the work done against air resistance. (4) MS: "Use of correct trigonometry to calculate Δh Or … component of g along slope [61.6 N] (1); Use of ΔEgrav = mgΔh [ΔEgrav = 90 kg × 9.81 × 215 m × sin 4.0° scores MP1&2] (1); Total work done = work done against gravity + work done against air resistance (1); Work against air resistance = 2.0 × 10⁴ J (allow ecf from (a)) (1) ['show that' value gives 1.68 × 10⁴ J]." Example: "Δh = 215 m × sin 4.0° = 15.0 m; ΔEgrav = 90 kg × 9.81 × 15.0 = 1.32 × 10⁴ J; W = 3.20 × 10⁴ J − 1.32 × 10⁴ J = 1.88 × 10⁴ J." ER: "Some candidates calculated the change in g.p.e. and left that as a final answer."
- (b)(ii) State one assumption made when calculating the work done against air resistance. (1) MS: "Force of gravity and air resistance are the only significant forces acting (to oppose the motion) Or Frictional forces (in the bearings of the bicycle) are negligible [accept zero, do not accept friction between bicycle and slope/ground] (1)." ER: "Many students decided that there should be no friction between the cyclist and the road, not realising that that would mean the road was too slippery for the cyclist to make any progress whatsoever."
- (c) At the top the road becomes horizontal and the cyclist pedals with the same force; explain how the speed changes. (2) MS: "No work done against (force of) gravity Or All work done against air resistance Or No backward force due to gravity so resultant force acts (1); Speed increases [MP2 dependent on MP1] (1)." ER: "many thinking that the force of gravity had a horizontal component, others getting confused between force, work and power."

### January 2023 (P71864A)

**[Jan23] Q1 (1 mark, spec 4) — Identify the scalar.** A displacement, B moment of a force, C power, D weight. **Answer C.** MS: "displacement is a vector … the moment of a force has a magnitude and a direction so is therefore a vector … weight is a force, and force is a vector."

**[Jan23] Q2 (1 mark, spec 10) — Gravitational field strength.** A weight per unit mass, B acceleration per unit mass, C gravitational potential energy per unit mass, D acceleration per unit weight. **Answer A.** MS: the other options "should be weight, not acceleration / gravitational potential energy / and mass, not weight".

**[Jan23] Q3 (1 mark, specs 32, 20) — Energy transfer for a compressed spring (VOIDED).** An object of mass m rests on a spring; the spring is compressed a further distance Δx by a vertical force F; the force is removed and the spring returns to its original length; when Δx becomes zero the object has vertical speed v. A ½FΔx = ½mv² − mgΔx; B ½FΔx = ½mv² + mgΔx; C FΔx = ½mv² − mgΔx; D FΔx = ½mv² + mgΔx. **MS gives B** (elastic energy ½FΔx = KE gained + gpe gained mgΔx; MS: "the total elastic energy is not FΔx" for C/D, "gpe should be added not subtracted" for A/C). **But the ER voided the item:** "This multiple-choice item was found to contain an error and has been removed from this report."

**[Jan23] Q4 (1 mark, spec 11) — Core practical 1: accurate g by free fall.** A student times a steel ball bearing falling a measured distance in air. Condition needed: A the air flow around the ball bearing should be laminar; B the time taken to achieve terminal velocity should be short; C the ball bearing should begin to fall before the timer is started; D air resistance and upthrust should both be negligible. **Answer D.** MS: A — "the drop speed would be too slow if the flow were laminar"; B — "the ball bearing should not be moving at terminal velocity at all"; C — "to calculate g you must know the initial velocity and the student isn't measuring that."

**[Jan23] Q5 (1 mark, spec 1) — SUVAT distance.** A racing car passes P at 35 m s⁻¹ (t = 0.0 s), accelerates uniformly, passes Q at 50 m s⁻¹ (t = 5.0 s). Distance s = ? A (50² − 35²)/(2 × 5); B (35 × 5) + (0.5 × 9.81 × 5²); C 0.5 × (35 + 50) × 5; D 35 + (9.81 × 5). **Answer C** (s = ½(u+v)t). MS: A — "the acceleration is not 5 m s⁻²"; B — "the acceleration is not 9.81 m s⁻²"; D — "not dimensionally correct".

**[Jan23] Q6 (1 mark, specs 6, 24) — Vector diagram for a wind-blown bubble.** Forces on a soap bubble: P (wind, horizontal), W (weight, down), U (upthrust, up). Four vector triangles combine P and (W − U) with resultant F. **Answer B.** MS: A "gives the force required for balance"; C "gives (W − U) − P"; D "gives P − (W − U)". ER: "Vectors are placed 'nose-to-tail' to find the resultant."

**[Jan23] Q7 (1 mark, specs 2, 3) — Displacement–time to acceleration–time graph.** A ball is thrown vertically up and caught; the displacement–time graph is an inverted parabola from 0 back to 0. Pick the matching a–t graph. **Answer B** (constant negative acceleration). MS: A — "the acceleration is not positive"; C — "the acceleration does not increase"; D — "the acceleration does not decrease." ER: "Negative acceleration gives a decreasing gradient of a displacement-time graph."

**[Jan23] Q8 (1 mark, specs 21, 19) — Time to lift a mass at given power.** A motor lifts 50 000 kg through 25 m; output power 700 kW. Time = ? A (50 000 × 25)/700 000; B (50 000 × 9.81 × 25)/700 000; C (50 000 × 25)/700; D (50 000 × 9.81 × 25)/700. **Answer B** (t = mgΔh/P). MS: A/C — "the mass has not been multiplied by g"; D — "the factor of 10³ is missing for the power."

**[Jan23] Q9 (1 mark, specs 31, 28) — Core practical 3: quantities to measure.** Masses are added to a copper wire and extensions measured. Which other quantities measured directly? A diameter and mass of the wire; B diameter and original length of the wire; C radius and mass; D radius and original length. **Answer B.** MS: "the mass is not needed"; "radius cannot be directly measured". ER: "Diameter is required to calculate stress and original length is required to calculate strain."

**[Jan23] Q10 (1 mark, spec 12) — Newton's third law pair.** A they act in different directions on the same body; B they are the same type of force and act on different bodies; C they have different magnitudes and act in different directions; D they are the same type of force and have different magnitudes. **Answer B.** MS: A — "the forces don't act on the same body"; C/D — "the forces have equal magnitudes".

**[Jan23] Q11 (3 marks, specs 7, 1) — The Stone and the Cliff.** A stone is projected horizontally from a cliff with initial horizontal velocity 3.8 m s⁻¹ from a height of 12 m. Calculate the horizontal distance to where it hits the ground. MS: "Use of s = ut + ½at² with u = 0 and a = g for flight time (1); Use of s = ut + ½at² with a = 0 for horizontal displacement of stone (1); Distance travelled = 5.9 m (1)." Example: "12 m = 0.5 × 9.81 m s⁻² × t²; t = √(12.0 ÷ 4.905) = 1.56 s; s = 3.8 m s⁻¹ × 1.56 s = 5.94 m." ER: "Others used a = g in the horizontal plane, or u = 3.8 m s⁻¹ in the vertical plane. Teaching students to set calculations out in two columns would help them to keep horizontal and vertical components clearly separated."

**[Jan23] Q12 (6 marks, specs 26, 24, 23, 25a) — The Falling Ball Experiment.** A student determines the viscosity of a liquid by the falling-ball method; at terminal velocity "drag force = weight of ball – upthrust"; the liquid's density is known; the student used a balance and a digital calliper.
- (a) Describe how the student could use her measurements to calculate the drag force. (4) MS: "(Use balance to measure) mass and multiply mass by g to determine weight Or Use the balance set to read newtons to determine weight (of ball) (1); Measure the diameter (of the ball) (with the calliper) to determine volume (1); Identify upthrust with weight of fluid displaced (1); Calculate the weight of fluid displaced by multiplying the volume of the ball by the density of the liquid and g (1)." ER: "The most common mark not awarded was for the explanation linking the weight of fluid displaced with the upthrust. It should be emphasised to students that calipers cannot directly measure the radius of a sphere; this was a common misconception."
- (b) The ball reached terminal velocity; flow was laminar. Calculate the viscosity. terminal velocity = 5.4 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹; radius of ball = 0.50 × 10⁻² m; drag force = 1.1 × 10⁻² N. (2) MS: "Use of F = 6πηrv (1); η = 2.2 × 10² Pa s (1)." Example: "1.1 × 10⁻² N = 6π × η × 0.50 × 10⁻² m × 5.4 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹; η = 1.1 × 10⁻² ÷ (6π × 2.7 × 10⁻⁶) = 216 Pa s." ER: "Very many students were unfamiliar with the unit for viscosity."

### 2. Recurring mark-scheme phrasing requirements

- **"Use of [formula]" marks**: nearly every calculation MP is phrased "Use of σ = F/A", "Use of p = mv", "Use of ΔW = FΔx", "Use of F = 6πηrv" — credit for substitution of physically correct values "although there may be conversion errors e.g. power of 10 error" (rule 4.4); "recall of the correct formula will be awarded when the formula is seen or implied by substitution" (Jan23 4.4).
- **Comparison-and-conclusion marks**: "Valid comparison in consistent units and conclusion" (Oct22 Q12(a); Jan23 Q14(b)(ii)); "Correct conclusion from valid comparison using student's calculated value" (Oct22 Q15(b)); "Correct conclusion based on comparison of candidate's momenta/speeds" (Oct22 Q16(b)); "Conclusion consistent with student's calculation" (Jan23 Q17(b)). The bare calculated value never earns the final mark.
- **ecf is pervasive**: "(ecf from (b)(i))" (Oct22 Q12(b)(ii), Q17(a)(ii); Jan23 Q14(b)(ii), Q15(b)(ii), Q18(a)(ii)); "allow ecf if method involves t from (a)" (Oct22 Q15(b)); "allow ecf from (a)" (Oct22 Q19(b)(i)).
- **"Do not accept" examples**: "Treat 'net force' as synonym for 'resultant force', so no mark" (Oct22 Q11(a)); "do not accept 'equal and opposite reaction'"; "One force is gravitational [do not accept 'weight']" (Oct22 Q18(b)); "Weight remains constant [do not accept 'mass']" (Oct22 Q17(b)); "do not accept 'viscous drag'" (Oct22 Q18(a)); "do not accept friction between bicycle and slope/ground" (Oct22 Q19(b)(ii)). Every MCQ distractor carries a printed reason ("X is incorrect because …").
- **Significant figures** (identical MS general notes): too many s.f. fine if it rounds to the MS value (3.1); too few s.f. loses the final mark in "show that" questions where "one more significant figure than the value in the question is needed" (3.2); g = 10 instead of 9.81 loses one mark, "not more than once per clip", 9.8 accepted (3.4); Oct22 Q12(b)(i) "[1.27 to 1.33][need to see third s.f.]".
- **Units**: "a missing or incorrect unit will normally mean that the final calculation mark will not be awarded" (2.1), not applied in show-that (2.2); both ERs report unit marks lost (Oct22 Q11(c), Q15(b); Jan23 Q15(b)(ii)).
- **Show-that rules**: reverse working earns both marks for a 2-mark show-that, only 2 of 3 for a 3-mark one (4.2/4.3; applied in Oct22 Q15(a)); "Bald (i.e. no working shown) correct answers may score full marks" (4.1) except show-that/extended calculations.
- **6-mark asterisk (QoWC) questions** share one table: 6 IC points → 4 IC marks + max 2 linkage marks (5 IC → 3+2; 4 → 3+1; 3 → 2+1; 2 → 2+0). IC points are written as paired "Or" alternatives with bracketed synonyms accepted.
- **Printed leniencies**: "allow P = Fv" (Oct22 Q11(c)); "9.7 cos 49° or 9.7 sin 41°" (Oct22 Q15(a)); "[allow diameter for radius]" (Oct22 Q17(a)(ii)); "Use the balance set to read newtons" (Jan23 Q12(a)); "[accept pivot point clearly indicated on diagram]" (Oct22 Q13(b)); "withhold one mark if more forces than three" (Oct22 Q18(a)). Jan23 adds graph-marking rules 6.1–6.4 (labelled axes with units, scale spreading points over more than half each axis, points plotted within 1 mm, thin best-fit line).

### 4. Question formats repeating across the two sessions

- **Terminal-velocity narrative (the signature repeat)**: Oct22 Q14(b) (6-mark IC/linkage, balloon rising) and Q11 (boat); Jan23 Q18(b) (3 marks, lift bag rising). Same chain each time: initial resultant force → acceleration → drag grows with speed → resultant force and acceleration fall to zero → constant/maximum velocity. Oct22 ER: "Terminal velocity situations are commonly asked about on exams so should be sufficiently covered."
- **Upthrust = weight of fluid displaced**: Oct22 Q14(a) (4 marks: U = ρVg, W = mg, resultant = U − W) and Jan23 Q18(a)(i) (5 marks: same chain, D = W − U) plus Jan23 Q12(a) (method description). The MS phrase "Identify upthrust with weight of fluid displaced" appears in both schemes.
- **Stokes' law**: calculation (Oct22 Q17(a)(ii) foram; Jan23 Q12(b) falling ball — both 2 marks, both with ecf) and conditions of validity (Oct22 Q3 MCQ; Jan23 Q18(a)(iii) two reasons).
- **Moments + centre of gravity**: Oct22 Q13 (wheelbarrow: explain via moments about G, then calculate x = 0.30 m) and Jan23 Q13 (stone on a step: define equilibrium and CoG, add force arrows, calculate P = 52 N). Both demand a stated pivot; both ERs report the same lost marks (pivot/geometry).
- **Momentum conservation in 1D + inelasticity**: Oct22 Q16 (state principle, test conservation numerically, explain KE loss) and Jan23 Q16(a) (zero initial momentum, find v with direction). Both sessions also pair it with Newton's 3rd law (Oct22 Q18(b), 5 marks; Jan23 Q10 MCQ + Q16(b)(i)) — "same type of force, different bodies" examined both times.
- **Young modulus from a graph**: Oct22 Q12 (F–x graph → gradient → k → E = kx/A, plus breaking-stress deduction) and Jan23 Q14 (stress–strain gradient, then suitability deduction). Both include "deduce whether…" questions with several accepted routes, both demand "valid comparison … and conclusion", and both ERs flag gradient technique ("use > half drawn line") and show-that sig figs.
- **Projectiles**: Oct22 Q15 (angled throw at a wall: show-that time, then deduce) and Jan23 Q11 (horizontal launch off a cliff, 3 marks). Both split into horizontal (a = 0) and vertical (a = g) SUVAT.
- **Work/energy/efficiency chain**: Oct22 Q19 (cyclist: ΔW = FΔs, mgΔh with sin θ, work against air resistance by difference, assumption, explain speed change) and Jan23 Q17 (High Striker: ΔW = FΔs, efficiency, mgΔh, deduce, then Ek ∝ v²). Both ERs name the same failures: no explicit final comparison; "work" not mentioned.
- **MCQ repeats**: vector/scalar identification (Q2/Q1), resultant vector diagram (Q7/Q6), motion-graph interpretation (Q1, Q4/Q7), efficiency and power (Q10/Q8), Young-modulus meaning/practical (Q5/Q9), Stokes' conditions (Q3/Q18(a)(iii)).
- **Structural repeats**: exactly one 6-mark QoWC asterisk question per paper (Oct22 Q14(b), Jan23 Q15(c)) with an identical IC/linkage table; a "Show that … about …" item in every paper (Oct22: 1.3 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹, 3 × 10⁴ J, 7 × 10⁻⁸ N, 0.8 s; Jan23: 2 × 10¹¹ Pa, about 300 N, about 200 N), each 2–5 marks under the reverse-working and sig-fig rules above; both papers end with a fluids/terminal-velocity question (Oct22 Q17 forams, Jan23 Q18 lift bag).
