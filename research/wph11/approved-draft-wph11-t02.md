## Density

Specification point: be able to use the equation density $\rho = \frac{m}{V}$

- **$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$** — The density of a sample = the mass of the sample ÷ the volume of the sample.
- The unit of density = $\text{kg m}^{-3}$.
- Density is a property of the material: the density does not change when the size or the shape of the sample changes.
- Stiffness is different: stiffness is a property of the object, so the stiffness of an object does change when the dimensions of the object change.
- **$V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$** — The volume of a sphere = $\frac{4}{3}$ × π × the radius cubed. Use the radius: if the question gives the diameter, halve it first ($r = \frac{d}{2}$).
- The volume of a cylinder = π × the radius squared × the length ($V = \pi r^2 l$); the volume of a rectangular block = length × width × height.
- If the question gives the weight $W$ of the sample, find the mass first: $m = \frac{W}{g}$.
- Convert cm³ to m³ by dividing by 1 000 000; convert mm to m by dividing by 1 000.
- Volume per second = mass per second ÷ the density of the fluid; volume per second also = the cross-sectional area × the speed of the flow ($Q = Av$).
- In multiple choice questions, check the units of every option: the wrong options usually carry the wrong units.
- For a floating object, the displaced volume = the submerged volume only, not the whole volume of the object.

## Upthrust and flotation

Specification point: understand how to use the relationship upthrust = weight of fluid displaced

- **Upthrust** is an upward force that a fluid exerts on an object placed in the fluid.
- Upthrust is caused by the displacement of the fluid.
- **$U = W_{\text{fluid displaced}}$** — The upthrust on an object = the weight of the fluid the object displaces.
- **$U = \rho_{\text{fluid}} \times V_{\text{disp}} \times g$** — The weight of displaced fluid = the density of the fluid × the displaced volume × g.
- For a fully submerged object, the displaced volume = the whole volume of the object ($V_{\text{disp}} = V_{\text{object}}$).
- A floating object is in equilibrium, so the resultant force on it = zero: the upthrust on the object = the weight of the object ($U = W$).
- A floating object displaces a weight of fluid equal to its own weight ($W_{\text{fluid displaced}} = W_{\text{object}}$).
- The fraction of a floating object that is submerged = the density of the object ÷ the density of the fluid ($\frac{V_{\text{sub}}}{V_{\text{object}}} = \frac{\rho_{\text{object}}}{\rho_{\text{fluid}}}$).
- In a denser liquid, a smaller displaced volume is enough to balance the same weight, so the object floats higher.
- If the fluid is less dense, a floating object must displace a larger volume, so that the weight of the displaced fluid still = the weight of the object.
- A hollow object floats because its overall (average) density is less than the density of the liquid.
- Free-body diagram of a floating object: one upward arrow labelled upthrust (or $U$), one downward arrow labelled weight (or $W$ or $mg$). Both arrows start from the same point on the object, and the two arrows are drawn the same length.
- Draw only the forces acting on the object: every extra force arrow loses one mark.
- The apparent weight of an object under water = the weight of the object − the upthrust on it ($W_{\text{app}} = W - U$).

**Object lowered into a liquid standing on a balance**

- The liquid exerts an upthrust on the object, upwards.
- By Newton's third law, the object exerts a force on the liquid that is equal in size and opposite in direction, downwards.
- That extra downward force on the liquid increases the reading on the balance.
- The increase in the balance reading = the weight of the displaced liquid.
- The mass of the displaced liquid = the increase in the balance reading; then $V = \frac{m}{\rho_{\text{liquid}}}$; then the mass of the object = $\rho_{\text{object}} \times V$.
- If the water is replaced by a lower-density liquid, the upthrust on the object is less, so the downward force of the object on the liquid is less, so the increase in the balance reading is less. The second mark depends on the first.

**Hydrometer**

- A hydrometer floating in a liquid is in equilibrium, so the upthrust on it = its weight ($U = W$).
- The weight of the hydrometer is the same in every liquid.
- In a denser liquid, each unit of displaced volume provides a greater weight of displaced liquid, so a smaller displaced volume is enough: the hydrometer floats higher.

**Submarine moving into different water**

- A submarine underwater is in equilibrium: the upthrust of the seawater on the submarine = the weight of the submarine.
- When the submarine moves into less dense water, the upthrust on it decreases, while the weight of the submarine does not change.
- The resultant force on the submarine now acts downwards, so the submarine begins to sink.
- To hold its depth, the submarine must reduce its weight by pumping water out.

**Lifting a submarine from the water**

- While the submarine is fully underwater, the tension in the crane cable = the weight of the submarine − the upthrust on it ($T = W - U$).
- As the submarine emerges from the water, the submerged volume decreases, so the upthrust on it decreases ($U = \rho_{\text{water}} \times V_{\text{sub}} \times g$).
- The weight of the submarine does not change.
- The tension in the cable therefore increases, until the submarine hangs clear of the water and the tension in the cable = the full weight of the submarine ($T = W$).

**Cartesian diver**

- Squeezing the bottle raises the pressure of the water, which forces water into the diver, so the volume of the trapped air decreases.
- The diver now displaces less water, so the upthrust on the diver decreases.
- The weight of the diver does not change.
- When the weight of the diver > the upthrust on the diver, the resultant force on the diver acts downwards.
- The diver therefore accelerates downwards and sinks.

**Galilean thermometer**

- When the temperature of the liquid rises, the liquid expands, so the density of the liquid falls.
- The upthrust on each bulb = the density of the liquid × the volume of the bulb × g, so the upthrust on each bulb falls.
- The weight of each bulb does not change.
- A bulb for which the weight of the bulb > the upthrust on the bulb now has a resultant force downwards, so that bulb sinks.

**Ballast tanks**

- Filling the ballast tanks increases the weight of the boat.
- The boat must displace more water so that the upthrust on it still = the weight of the boat ($U = W$), so the boat sits lower.
- The centre of gravity of the displaced water moves lower, which makes the boat more stable.

**Iceberg**

- An iceberg floats in equilibrium: the weight of the iceberg = the weight of the displaced seawater.
- So the submerged fraction of the iceberg = the density of ice ÷ the density of seawater.
- In less dense water, a greater volume must be displaced to balance the same weight, so the iceberg sits deeper.

## Viscosity, Stokes' law and terminal velocity

Specification point: a) be able to use the equation for viscous drag (Stokes' law), $F = 6\pi\eta rv$  b) understand that this equation applies only to small spherical objects moving at low speeds with laminar flow (or in the absence of turbulent flow) and that viscosity is temperature dependent

- **Laminar flow** — the layers of the fluid slide past each other without mixing, and the velocity of the fluid at a fixed point stays constant.
- **Turbulent flow** — the layers of the fluid mix and the flow is chaotic.
- **Viscosity** measures the resistance of a fluid to flow; the unit of viscosity = Pa s.
- As the temperature of a liquid increases, the viscosity of the liquid decreases; as the temperature decreases, the viscosity increases.
- **$D = 6\pi\eta r v$** — Stokes' law: the viscous drag on a sphere = 6 × π × the viscosity of the fluid × the radius of the sphere × the speed of the sphere. (The specification prints this drag as $F$.)
- Stokes' law applies only when all of these hold: the object is small, the object is spherical, the speed is low, and the flow is laminar (there is no turbulent flow).
- List questions often cap the answer at two conditions: the safest pair = a small spherical object, and laminar flow (or a low speed).
- When a question asks for "one other condition", look at what the stem already gives and state a different one.
- The drag on a falling object increases as the speed increases ($D \propto v$): this is why a falling object stops accelerating.
- While the sphere sinks, the upthrust on it stays constant, because the volume of fluid it displaces does not change ($U = \rho_{\text{fluid}} \times V \times g$, with $V$ constant).
- <u>Terminal velocity</u> = the constant maximum velocity reached by an object falling through a fluid.

**Sinking at terminal velocity**

- Three forces act on a sphere sinking through a fluid: the weight of the sphere downwards, the upthrust of the fluid on the sphere upwards, and the drag of the fluid on the sphere upwards.
- A sinking object at terminal velocity has zero resultant force ($W = U + D$).
- The weight of the sphere downwards is balanced by the upthrust plus the drag upwards.
- The drag on the sphere is therefore the difference between the weight and the upthrust ($D = W - U$).
- To calculate the terminal velocity: $D = W - U$ first, then $v = \frac{D}{6\pi\eta r}$.

**Reaching terminal velocity**

- At the moment of release, the speed of the sphere is zero, so the drag on it is zero.
- At first the weight of the sphere is greater than the upthrust plus the drag, so the resultant force on the sphere acts downwards, and the sphere accelerates.
- As the speed of the sphere increases, the drag on it increases ($D = 6\pi\eta r v$), while the weight and the upthrust on it stay the same.
- The resultant force downwards becomes smaller, so the acceleration becomes smaller.
- The velocity stops changing when the drag has grown enough that the upthrust plus the drag = the weight ($U + D = W$).
- On a velocity-time graph, the gradient = the acceleration: the curve starts steep, becomes less steep as the drag builds up, and levels off horizontal at the terminal velocity.
- In a vacuum there is no drag and no upthrust on the object, so the resultant force on it = its weight, and the gradient of the velocity-time graph stays constant.

**Deducing whether Stokes' law applies**

- Calculate the real drag from the force balance: $D = W - U$, with $U = \rho_{\text{fluid}} \times \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \times g$.
- Calculate the drag Stokes' law predicts at the measured speed: $D = 6\pi\eta r v$, with $r = \frac{d}{2}$.
- Compare the two values, then finish with a conclusion that follows from your numbers.
- The same method works in reverse: calculate the speed Stokes' law predicts ($v = \frac{D}{6\pi\eta r}$) and compare it with the measured speed.
- Every "deduce" and "assess" answer ends the same way: a comparison of two calculated values followed by a conclusion in words.

**Sphere size and terminal velocity**

- The weight of a sphere is proportional to $r^3$ and the upthrust on it is proportional to $r^3$, but the drag on it at a given speed is proportional to $r$ only ($W \propto r^3$, $U \propto r^3$, $D \propto r$).
- A larger sphere falls faster, because the forces balance at a higher speed.
- The terminal velocity of a sphere is proportional to the square of its radius ($v_t \propto r^2$).
- When terminal velocity is measured for spheres of different diameters, a graph of $v$ against $d^2$ is a straight line through the origin.

**Rising objects**

- The drag on an object always acts in the direction opposite to its motion: downwards on a falling object, upwards on a rising object.
- A rising bubble has the upthrust acting upwards and the drag acting downwards on it; the weight of the bubble is negligible.
- A rising bubble that grows: the upthrust on it grows with $r^3$ while the drag on it grows with $r$, so the bubble speeds up.

**Balloon rising through the atmosphere**

- On the ground, the upthrust of the air on the balloon is greater than the weight of the balloon, so the resultant force on it acts upwards, and it accelerates.
- The balloon expands as it rises, until it reaches its full volume.
- Above that point, the volume of the balloon is fixed while the air gets less dense, so the upthrust on the balloon falls ($U = \rho_{\text{air}} \times V \times g$).
- When the upthrust on the balloon < the weight of the balloon, the resultant force on it acts downwards, so the balloon slows down.
- The balloon stops rising when the upthrust on it has fallen to equal its weight ($U = W$).

**A sphere sinking into colder, deeper water**

- Start: a sphere sinking through seawater at terminal velocity, so the weight of the sphere = the upthrust on it + the drag on it ($W = U + D$).
- Deeper water is colder, and the viscosity of water increases as its temperature falls, so the viscosity of the water around the sphere is greater.
- So the drag on the sphere at any given speed is larger ($D = 6\pi\eta r v$: a larger $\eta$ gives a larger $D$ at the same $v$).
- Deeper water is also denser, so the upthrust on the sphere is larger ($U = \rho_{\text{water}} \times V \times g$: a larger $\rho$ gives a larger $U$).
- The weight of the sphere does not change.
- At the old terminal velocity, the upthrust plus the drag on the sphere is now greater than the weight of the sphere, so the resultant force on the sphere acts upwards.
- The sphere slows down until the forces balance again, at a lower speed: the terminal velocity of the sphere is lower in the deeper water.

- <span style="color:#b91c1c">Not accepted:</span> "small" on its own when the stem already states the sphere is small; "the flow around the sphere was turbulent" offered as a measurement error; "the densities used were incorrect"; "mass" written where the mark scheme wants the weight.

## Hooke's law and stiffness

Specification point: be able to use the Hooke's law equation, $\Delta F = k\Delta x$, where $k$ is the stiffness of the object

- **$\Delta F = k\Delta x$** — Hooke's law: the change in the force on a spring = the stiffness of the spring × the change in its extension.
- The symbol $k$ = the stiffness constant of the object, also called the spring constant.
- The unit of stiffness = $\text{N m}^{-1}$ (or $\text{N cm}^{-1}$ when the lengths are in cm).
- $k$ is a property of the object: the same metal made into a longer or thicker wire has a different stiffness.
- The Young modulus is a property of the material, so it does not change with the dimensions of the sample.
- The extension of a spring = the new length − the original length ($\Delta x = x - x_0$). Use the difference, never the final length alone.
- With two loads on one spring, the stiffness = the change in the force ÷ the change in the extension, using the differences between the two states.
- Two-step spring problems use $F = k\Delta x$ twice: first find $k$ from a known load and its extension, then apply that $k$ to the new load.
- On a force-extension graph, the stiffness = the gradient of the straight section.
- On a force-length graph, convert first: the stiffness = the force ÷ (the final length − the original length).
- On a curved force-extension graph, the stiffness at a point = the gradient of the tangent at that point.
- **$k = \frac{EA}{x}$** — The stiffness of a wire = the Young modulus of the material × the cross-sectional area ÷ the original length. A longer wire has a smaller stiffness, because the extension at a given force is proportional to the length.
- Springs in series: the same force passes through each spring, and the extensions add ($k_{\text{total}} = \frac{k}{2}$ for two identical springs).
- Springs in parallel: each spring extends by the same amount and the springs share the load; the spring constants add ($k_{\text{total}} = 2k$ for two identical springs).
- A load resting on four identical corner springs puts $\frac{W}{4}$ on each spring.
- Hooke's law holds only up to the <u>limit of proportionality</u>: beyond it, the force is no longer proportional to the extension, and the law no longer applies.

## Stress, strain and the Young modulus

Specification point: understand how to use the relationships (tensile or compressive) stress = force / cross-sectional area, (tensile or compressive) strain = change in length / original length, Young modulus = stress / strain

- **$\sigma = \frac{F}{A}$** — The stress on a sample = the force ÷ the cross-sectional area; the unit = the pascal (Pa) = $\text{N m}^{-2}$. The same relationship holds for tensile and compressive stress.
- **$\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta x}{x}$** — The strain of a sample = the change in length ÷ the original length. Strain has no unit; a percentage is accepted.
- **$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}$** — The Young modulus = the stress ÷ the strain; the unit = Pa.
- The Young modulus is a property of the material alone: it is the same for a short wire and a long wire of the same material.
- Units to keep straight: density $\text{kg m}^{-3}$; stress and Young modulus Pa ($\text{N m}^{-2}$); stiffness $\text{N m}^{-1}$; viscosity Pa s; strain no unit.
- The usual calculation route: $A = \pi r^2$ with $r = \frac{d}{2}$; $\sigma = \frac{F}{A}$; $\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta x}{x}$; $E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}$. Each step earns its own mark.
- From a stress-strain graph, the Young modulus = the gradient of the initial straight section; draw a large triangle covering more than half of the line.
- From a force-extension graph, the Young modulus = the gradient × the original length ÷ the area ($E = \frac{k \times x_0}{A}$, since the gradient = $k$).
- From a mass-extension graph, the gradient is in $\text{kg m}^{-1}$, so multiply it by $g$: the Young modulus = gradient × g × the original length ÷ the area.
- **$\Delta x = \frac{F x_0}{A E}$** — The extension of a wire under a force: longer and thinner gives a larger extension; shorter and thicker gives a smaller extension; $E$ does not change.
- Two wires of the same material under the same force: doubling the area halves the extension ($\Delta x \propto \frac{1}{A}$); doubling the original length doubles the extension ($\Delta x \propto x_0$).
- Measure the diameter with a micrometer screw gauge (or digital callipers), at several positions along the wire and in several orientations, then average before computing the area.
- A vernier scale has a better resolution (0.1 mm) than a millimetre rule, so the percentage uncertainty in the reading is smaller.
- Reduce parallax: place the rule against the wire, use a set square between the rule and the wire, and read at right angles to the scale.
- A missing or wrong unit normally loses the final calculation mark.
- In compression, the length decreases, so the change in length = the original length − the final length; in tension, it = the final length − the original length.

## Force-extension graphs and deformation

Specification point: a) be able to draw and interpret force-extension and force-compression graphs  b) understand the terms limit of proportionality, elastic limit, yield point, elastic deformation and plastic deformation and be able to apply them to these graphs

- <u>Limit of proportionality</u> — the point beyond which the force is no longer proportional to the extension; the end of the straight-line section.
- <u>Elastic limit</u> — the greatest force or stress up to which the object returns to its original length when the load is removed; beyond it, the deformation is permanent.
- <u>Yield point</u> — the point at which the material begins to undergo sudden or large plastic deformation; on the graph, it is where the curve flattens while the strain keeps increasing.
- The three named points are different, and they come in this order: limit of proportionality first, then elastic limit, then yield point.
- <u>Elastic deformation</u> — the object returns to its original size, shape and length when the force is removed.
- <u>Plastic deformation</u> — the object stays permanently deformed; it does not return to its original length.
- The work done stretching a sample = the area under the force-extension graph, and that area = the elastic strain energy stored.
- Loading and unloading curves drawn with different gradients mean the stiffness of the object has changed.
- <span style="color:#b91c1c">Not accepted:</span> "a greater Young modulus" as the reason for a changed gradient: the Young modulus belongs to the material and has not changed.
- The area between the loading and unloading lines = the energy dissipated (the hysteresis loss, for example in a rubber band).
- If the original length was forgotten when plotting, the graph keeps the same shape but starts at a positive extension.
- On a force-length graph, convert to extension before using it: for a force rising from zero to R while the length goes from p to q, the work done = ½ × R × (q − p).
- A wire stretched beyond its elastic limit does not return to its original length when the force is removed: it is permanently (plastically) deformed.

## Stress-strain graphs and breaking stress

Specification point: be able to draw and interpret tensile or compressive stress-strain graphs, and understand the term breaking stress

- <u>Breaking stress</u> — the maximum stress a material can carry before it fractures; also called the ultimate tensile stress or ultimate tensile strength.
- The force at fracture = the breaking stress × the cross-sectional area ($F = \sigma_{\text{break}} \times A$).
- The gradient of the initial straight section of a stress-strain graph = the Young modulus; a steeper initial gradient means a stiffer material with a larger $E$.
- The area under a stress-strain graph = the work done per unit volume, not the work done; the energy = the area × the volume of the sample.
- A large plastic region means the material is ductile; a graph that fractures soon after the linear section, with little plastic deformation, means the material is brittle.
- Compare-and-contrast marks come from: the initial gradient, the way the gradient changes, the breaking stress, the breaking strain, and the area under the graph.
- Concrete is stronger in compression than in tension: quote the two breaking stresses from the graph and compare them.
- Reading a stress off the graph first and then multiplying by the area is the expected route for fracture-force questions.

**Pre-tensioned concrete**

- Steel rods are stretched, the concrete is poured around them and sets, and then the rods are released.
- The stretched rods pull the concrete together, so the concrete starts with a built-in compressive stress.
- When a tensile load is applied, the applied tension must first remove the built-in compression, so the concrete itself still carries no tension.
- The steel rods carry the tensile load, because steel withstands a larger tensile stress than concrete.
- If the rods are stretched beyond their elastic limit, they are permanently deformed: the pre-compression is lost, and the concrete can no longer carry as much tension.

**When processing changes a material**

- When processing changes a material (for example concrete drying, which increases its Young modulus), calculate the Young modulus from the gradient of each linear region, then compare the two values as a ratio.

## Elastic strain energy

Specification point: be able to calculate the elastic strain energy $E_{\text{el}}$ in a deformed material sample, using the equation $\Delta E_{\text{el}} = \frac{1}{2}F\Delta x$, and from the area under the force-extension graph. The estimation of area and hence energy change for both linear and non-linear force-extension graphs is expected.

- **$\Delta E_{\text{el}} = \frac{1}{2}F\Delta x$** — The elastic strain energy stored in a sample = ½ × the final force × the extension.
- For a spring that obeys Hooke's law, also $\Delta E_{\text{el}} = \frac{1}{2}k\Delta x^2$.
- Substituting $\Delta x = \frac{F}{k}$ gives the third useful form: $\Delta E_{\text{el}} = \frac{F^2}{2k}$.
- The energy stored = the area under the force-extension graph.
- The change in energy between two loads = the trapezium area: ½ × (the sum of the two forces) × the change in extension.
- For a non-linear graph, estimate the area by counting squares, or by splitting it into rectangles and triangles.
- Under a stress-strain graph, the area = the energy per unit volume: the energy = the area × the volume of the sample.
- To compare the strain energies of two materials, compare the areas under their graphs up to the same stress.

**Energy transfers**

- When a spring launches an object, the elastic strain energy stored in the spring becomes the kinetic energy of the object.
- When the object also changes height, include the work done by gravity: the elastic strain energy = the kinetic energy gained + the gravitational potential energy gained ($\Delta E_{\text{el}} = \Delta E_k + \Delta E_{\text{grav}}$).
- The energy stored in a compressed spring can be greater than the work done by the person compressing it, because gravity has also done work on it during the compression.
- Some questions compare the gravitational potential energy lost with the elastic strain energy stored: calculate both, compare the values, and give a conclusion.

## Core Practical 2 — falling-ball viscometer

Specification point: CORE PRACTICAL 2: use a falling-ball method to determine the viscosity of a liquid

**Method**

- Fill a tall measuring cylinder with the liquid and wrap two or more rubber bands around the cylinder to act as markers.
- The top marker sits far enough below the surface that the sphere has already reached terminal velocity when the timing begins.
- Measure the distance between the markers with a metre rule, and the time between them with a stopwatch.
- The terminal velocity = the distance between the markers ÷ the time ($v = \frac{s}{t}$).
- Repeat the timing and use the mean. Alternative: time different distances and take the gradient of a distance-time graph.
- A data logger or motion sensor can record the fall automatically; on a displacement-time graph, the terminal velocity = the gradient of the straight section.

**Calculation**

- At terminal velocity, the weight of the sphere = the upthrust on it + the drag on it ($W = U + D$).
- The weight of the sphere: $W = mg$.
- The upthrust on the sphere = the weight of the displaced liquid: $U = \rho_{\text{liquid}} \times \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \times g$.
- The drag on the sphere: $D = 6\pi\eta r v$.
- Rearrange the balance equation to find the viscosity $\eta$.
- In the balance equation, $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\rho_{\text{sphere}} g$ = the weight of the sphere; $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\rho_{\text{liquid}} g$ = the weight of the displaced liquid (the upthrust on the sphere); $6\pi\eta r v$ = the viscous drag on the sphere.

**Errors and validity**

- A larger sphere has a greater terminal velocity, so the time between the markers is shorter; the stopwatch uncertainty stays the same, so the percentage uncertainty is greater.
- A measured viscosity lower than the accepted value can come from: the liquid being warmer than stated, the diameter measured too small, the time measured too short, or the marker distance measured too large.
- <span style="color:#b91c1c">Not accepted:</span> "the flow around the sphere was turbulent", "the densities used were incorrect", "the temperature of the room".
- Keep the temperature of the liquid constant during the measurement, because the viscosity of a liquid depends on its temperature.
- A wide cylinder is accepted as a validity condition: the sphere should not fall close to the walls.

## Core Practical 3 — Young modulus of a wire

Specification point: CORE PRACTICAL 3: determine the Young modulus of a material

**Method**

- Measure the original length of the wire with a metre rule, from the clamp to the marker.
- Hang a small mass on the wire first, so the wire is straight and taut before the original length is measured.
- Measure the diameter with a micrometer screw gauge (or digital callipers), at several positions along the wire and in several orientations, then use the mean.
- You measure the initial length and the extended length and subtract: writing "measure the extension" as a method point <span style="color:#b91c1c">scores nothing</span>.
- Add masses in steps; after each addition, read the marker position: the extension = the new length − the original length.
- Wear safety goggles, because the wire could snap.
- Plot force against extension, or convert the readings to stress and strain and plot stress against strain.

**Analysis**

- The Young modulus = the gradient of the linear section of the graph; use a large triangle covering more than half of the drawn line.
- From a force-extension graph: the Young modulus = the gradient × the original length ÷ the area.
- From a graph of added mass against extension: the Young modulus = the gradient × g × the original length ÷ the area.
- Long and thin wires give a larger extension for the same load, need smaller loads, and give a smaller percentage uncertainty in the extension.
- A shorter wire gives a steeper force-extension graph, but the calculated Young modulus is the same, because $E$ is a property of the material.
- The cross-sectional area: $A = \pi r^2 = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}$. Average the diameter readings before squaring.

## Mark scheme conventions

- A line beginning "Use of" earns the substitution mark for that equation, even when the final value is wrong.
- Every mark point ends with its own (1); the last mark of a calculation is usually the final answer with the correct unit.
- A missing or wrong unit normally costs the final calculation mark.
- Error carried forward: a wrong value from an earlier part, used correctly later, still earns the later marks.
- Some final marks are dependent marks: they only score if an earlier point has been made.
- "Or" joins alternatives for the same single mark: any one of the printed wordings scores.
- Deduce and assess questions: calculate, compare with the given value, then state a conclusion that follows from your own numbers. An inconsistent conclusion loses the last mark.
- "Show that" answers need one more significant figure than the printed value; reverse working on a three-mark show-that keeps only two marks.
- Use $g = 9.81$; using 10 costs one mark, once per paper; 9.8 is accepted.
- Graph-read answers come with printed tolerance ranges; the graph mark usually depends on the reading being taken first.
- Six-mark starred questions award four marks for content and two for structure: write linked full sentences in a logical order, and include the named required points.
- Multiple choice: every wrong option has a printed physics reason; learn the reasons, because the same misconceptions are recycled as distractors every series.
